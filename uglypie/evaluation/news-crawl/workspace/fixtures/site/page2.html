Valve Maintenance Checklist

Reliable irrigation begins with valves that open fully, close cleanly, and respond to the controller without delay. Crews should inspect each valve box for standing water, loose wiring, cracked fittings, and signs of insects. The inspection should happen before the first heavy watering period because a small leak can become expensive once the system is under daily use.

Every check should include a manual open and close cycle. Listen for chatter, watch the pressure gauge, and confirm that downstream emitters start evenly. When a valve opens slowly, debris may be trapped near the diaphragm. When it closes slowly, the bleed path may be blocked. Both problems can be fixed quickly if the crew carries seals, brushes, and replacement solenoids.

Documentation keeps maintenance from becoming guesswork. Record the zone name, valve model, measured pressure, repair parts, and the worker who performed the inspection. If the same zone fails repeatedly, the notes will show whether the problem is a bad valve, poor filtration, or pressure that is too high for the installed hardware.

After repairs, run a short irrigation cycle and walk the first and last lateral lines. The far end of the zone should receive water within the expected time window. If it does not, the issue may be a partially closed isolation valve or a blocked line. Finishing with this field check prevents false confidence from a controller screen that only reports command status.

Seasonal shutdown should include one final valve exercise and a review of all open issues. Mark boxes that need risers, clear vegetation around lids, and store spare parts where they can be found quickly. These habits reduce emergency calls and keep water delivery predictable when crops need it most.

For teams with rotating staff, a short checklist is better than a long manual. It captures the essential observations without slowing the field crew, and it makes each repair easier for the next person to understand.
